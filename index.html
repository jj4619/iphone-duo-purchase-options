The things worth reading before anything else

The lease looks competitive right up until you ask what you own

On plain cash the Apple lease sits mid pack, a few dollars either side of the company rows. That comparison is missing the thing that matters most. At month 24 you hand the phone back and hold nothing. Take the company route and the same month leaves you owning a Duo outright, worth about $1,139, free to sell or trade toward whatever is next.

Net that off and the ordering inverts. By the two year mark the company route has cost you less than nothing, because the 15 Pro sale and the phone in your hand together cover more than you put in. The Verizon route has cost around $539 and the lease $1,097. The lease is not a slightly worse option, it is the worst on the page, because every dollar you paid bought use rather than equity.

This is why the ranking weights both equally rather than picking one. On cash alone the lease would sit above the Verizon rows, which is plainly wrong for a path that ends with nothing in your hand. On net cost alone the cash you actually had to find each month disappears. Blended, the lease lands where it belongs, near the bottom.

The real credit kills the filler phone trick on economics, not just timing

Verizon advertised up to $1,020 upgrading a line. The credit an actual three year old Pro attracts is $660. That gap is the whole story: the tiers are graded by what the device is worth, not flat across a broad category, and a 15 Pro already sits well down the ladder.

Paths 03 and 04 needed the opposite to be true. Their entire premise was that a cheap handset collects the same credit as a good one, so you sell the good one and pocket the difference. If a 15 Pro only fetches $660, a phone cheap enough to buy as a filler fetches far less, and both paths now finish behind simply handing Verizon your own phone.

I have left them on the page with a slider so you can test it. Drag the filler credit up and watch where the crossover sits. At the default $400 they lose by $20 and $60 respectively, and that is before the 60 day rule, the two shipping cycles and the inspection you cannot appeal.

Your cracked back is sitting in front of roughly $510

Verizon's trade in terms say condition is judged on whether the device powers on, whether the battery is damaged, and whether there are front or back screen cracks. Your back is cracked. If an inspector applies that rule literally, the promo value collapses to plain market value and the $660 quote goes with it.

A back glass repair converts that coin flip into a near certainty, and you can now get one for as little as $50 through a mail in service. Flip the crack toggle to No and watch what happens to path 01.

The 60 day rule probably kills the filler phone trick

Verizon requires that a traded in device has been active on your account for 60 consecutive days before the trade for many existing customer promos. The Duo ships October 23. A phone you buy today lands well inside that window, so a filler phone bought now would very likely be rejected for the promo tier.

Your 15 Pro has been on the line for years and clears it easily. That rule was already the main obstacle to paths 03 and 04. With the credit for a 15 Pro coming in well under the advertised ceiling, the economics have failed too, so those two are dead twice over rather than once.

The company plan is quietly the most flexible thing here

The phone pays off over 24 months and is yours from then on. No promo credits to forfeit, no carrier agreement, no buyout to fund at the end. That is a full year earlier than any Verizon path and it arrives with real equity rather than the lease's clean but empty exit.

It also costs nothing to start. The 15 Pro sale more than covers the tax and any repair, so the upfront column runs negative on all four company rows. At two years the phone puts its full resale value toward whatever comes next, while the Verizon paths at that same moment contribute $133.

With the Verizon credit landing at $660 rather than the advertised $1,020, the company plan now wins on price as well. Every company row finishes below every Verizon row, so the trade that used to be cheaper but more restrictive is now simply worse on both counts.

The one thing left to establish is what leaving inside those 24 months costs you on the remaining balance. At twelve months that is roughly $685 on a Duo.

The Verizon advantage does not exist until year three

Credits arrive monthly and you forfeit the rest the moment you change plans or leave. At twelve months you have banked a third of them, so the gap between financing through Verizon and just buying the phone is almost nothing. By month 36 it is the full credit. Upgrading at twelve months costs you money on six of these thirteen paths

If a phone you want more shows up next autumn, the Duo has to help pay for it. On the Verizon paths it does the opposite. At month 12 you would settle a device balance of about $1,333 and forfeit two thirds of a credit you have only partly received, against a phone worth about $1,479. The old phone leaves you several hundred dollars in the hole before you have spent a cent on the new one.

Buy it outright, or take either company route, and the same moment hands you the phone's full resale value toward whatever is next. That two thousand dollar swing is what the carrier subsidy actually costs, and it never appears in a price comparison. Switch the year table to the last view and read the year one column.

The Apple lease buyout is priced below what the phone will be worth

Apple sets the payoff at retail minus what you have already paid, rather than at projected market value the way a normal lease does. At month 24 that makes the buyout roughly $607 on a phone modelled to be worth about $1,139.

So paths 08 and 09 are the same contract read two different ways. Returning it costs you about $1,477 and leaves you with nothing. Buying it out costs exactly what buying outright costs, with the money spread over two years at zero percent. Returning is almost never the right call.

Where the numbers come from. Duo pricing and dates are from Apple's September 9 announcement and product pages. Verizon advertised up to $1,200 with a new myPlan line and $1,020 upgrading an existing one at the iPhone 18 Pro launch, applied as bill credits across 36 months. Those are ceilings for a current flagship trade-in. The model runs on $660 for a three year old Pro, and both that and the filler phone figure sit on sliders. Apple's trade in maximum for a clean iPhone 15 Pro was revised down to $370 on September 9. The Apple Upgrade lease rate comes from Apple's own $57.99 per month figure for the 256GB Duo on a 24 month term, and its published rule that the buyout equals retail minus what you have paid. Back glass repair at Apple is $169 for the 15 Pro, battery service is $99. The tax presets are illustrative rates covering a typical metro spread, and the slider takes any figure.

Where I am guessing. Resale values run off a single slider you set yourself, anchored on a 256GB 15 Pro with the crack fixed, the battery still at 79%, sold privately. The default of $470 is built from Swappa's September listings, where a 15 Pro starts at $438 for the cheapest 128GB in fair condition. The adjustments around it, $90 off for a cracked back, $30 on for a genuine Apple panel, $60 on for a fresh battery, and 79% of private for an instant buyback, are my estimates rather than quotes. Every Duo resale figure is a model, since the phone does not ship until October 23. So are the $30 genuine panel premium, the $80 and $50 repair prices, the $180 filler phone, the $150 market value fallback, and the $20 monthly cost of an added Verizon line. Lease rates above 256GB are scaled from Apple's published rate.

On the year 2 cost column. It measures at month 24 rather than 36, because that is where the paths diverge most and where the lease ends. It assumes you could sell the phone at the modelled resale value that month, which for a first generation foldable is the softest assumption on the page. Switch the depreciation control to the foldable curve to see how much of the company advantage survives a weaker used market.

Not modelled. Any service included with a company plan, which at typical carrier rates would be worth more over three years than every other line on this page combined. Also excluded: the $79 case, the $129 folio, the charger Apple no longer includes, early termination fees, and the possibility that a corporate liable company handset is never yours to sell.
